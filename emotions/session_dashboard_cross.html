Cross-Model Analysis

Emotion concepts across 8 open models · replication of Sofroniew et al. 2026 · updated 2026-06-04

Part A · Characterization
Part B · Behavior
  • What this is: the same pipeline on 8 open-weight models (Gemma-4, Qwen3, Llama-3.1/3.3), compared against the paper's results on Claude.
  • How: one emotion vector per emotion per model — its average middle-layer activation over short emotion stories, denoised against neutral text — then the paper's own tests. We compare results only; every number is the model's own.
  • Part A in one line: how models represent emotion carries over well; reading an emotion off a single word is weaker and model-dependent.
  • Verdicts: green matches the paper · amber partly · red no.
★ Key findings — across all 8 models

Bottom line: how these models represent emotion replicates broadly; the weak spot is the paper's single-word read — on smaller models one word is too noisy, and averaging the passage recovers it.

vs Claude: the geometry is about as clean as the paper's; the gap is the single-word read. Claude concentrates the emotion on that one word; these smaller models spread it across the passage — so user-vs-reply fails on all 8, and the ':' beats the user's word on only 2/8.

  • Emotions form a human-like map on every model. The top axis is always valence (unpleasant↔pleasant), matching human ratings on 8/8 (r 0.70–0.78); similar emotions cluster, opposites point apart (Fig 5–8).
  • The reply's emotion is set before the model speaks. Readable at the Assistant ':' on all 8; it clearly beats the user's own words on only 2/8 (Fig 11).
  • Emotion is tracked per speaker, not per name-tag — on all 8/8: relabel Human / Assistant / Person 1 / 2 and the vector barely moves (Fig 19).
  • Each vector points at on-theme words, in many languages — afraid → fear, 恐惧, страх — on all 8 (Table 1, logit lens).
  • The geometry transfers; the single-word read does not. At one word the emotion is weak and model-dependent (scenario 5/8; user-vs-reply 0/8) — it is there in the passage, just not readable from any single word (Fig 2 / 10).
  • Differences track family, not size. Only Llama puts the calm↔excited axis at #2; Gemma can't read it from a single word; a 4B edge model is as clean as a 70B. None matches Claude across the board (Llama is closest on representation).

What each column asks:
• Scenario lights up its emotion: at one word, does an implicit story ("my flight was cancelled") activate its emotion's vector? (of 12)
• Intensity moves the right way: do "more vs less intense" cues shift the emotion the predicted way? (of 12; ≈6/12 is chance)
• Valence is the #1 axis: correlation of the biggest axis with human pleasant↔unpleasant ratings
• Calm↔excited axis: which axis is arousal, and how strong (the paper finds it #2)
• User ≠ reply emotion: can the user's feeling be told from the reply's? (paper: yes, r ≈ 0.11; a high r means NOT separable)
• Reply emotion at ':': does ':' predict the reply better than the user's word? (green = ':' clearly wins; amber = predicts but no better)
• Per-speaker, not name-tag: own-relabel cosine (high = ignores the name) / own-vs-other cosine (low = distinct)

3 · By family, and vs Claude

  • Llama (3.1-8B / 3.1-70B / 3.3-70B) — most paper-faithful: the only family with arousal on PC2, the cleanest single-word read, the most legible logit lens. 3.1-70B and 3.3-70B are near-identical fine-tune twins (~0.99 cosine).
  • Qwen (3-8B / 3-32B) — single-word reads work: Fig 2 is clean, arousal on a deeper axis. Only Qwen3-32B loses the ':'-over-user edge.
  • Gemma (4-31B / E4B / 26B-A4B) — strong geometry, weak single-word reads: Fig 2 fails (0–2/12), arousal on a deep PC4; dense, edge and MoE are indistinguishable, so family beats architecture and size.
  • vs Claude: Claude shows the full set — clean geometry and the single-word reads. None of the 8 matches it across the board; Llama is closest.

Fair-comparison note. The paper studied Claude (frontier, internal evals); we use 8 open-weight models, public data, and read at a single word rather than averaging the passage — so the gaps most likely reflect scale and method, not a flaw in the paper. (Claude's own 1–7 ratings match human valence/arousal norms at 0.93 / 0.91, Fig 58 — why they serve as the human stand-in.)
