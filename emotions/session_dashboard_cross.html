Cross-Model Analysis

Emotion concepts across 8 open models · replication of Sofroniew et al. 2026 · updated 2026-06-03

Part A · Characterization
Part B · Behavior

In one line: we ran the identical pipeline on 8 open-weight models (Gemma-4, Qwen3, Llama-3.1/3.3) and compared the results against the paper, which studied Claude. The paper has two halves. It first characterizes the emotion representations (what they are, how they form, how they are read off), then shows how they drive behavior (preferences, and misaligned actions like reward hacking). This page mirrors that split: Part A · Characterization (here) and Part B · Behavior (next). Part A in short: the way models represent emotions carries over well; reading an emotion off a single word is weaker and depends on the model.

The setup is identical for every model. For each one we extract one "emotion vector" per emotion (its average middle-layer activation over short emotion-evoking stories, denoised against neutral text), then run the paper's tests, so this page compares results only. Verdicts: green = matches the paper · amber = partly · red = no. Every number is the model's own; per-model method details live on each model's page.

★ Key findings — across all 8 models

Bottom line: the way these models represent emotions replicates broadly; the weak, model-specific part is reading an emotion off a single token.

  • Emotions form a human-like map on every model. The biggest axis is always valence (unpleasant↔pleasant), matching human ratings on 8/8 (r 0.70–0.78, Fig 7–8).
  • The reply's emotion is set before the model speaks. Readable at the Assistant ':' on all 8; it clearly beats the user's own words on only 2/8 (Fig 11).
  • Emotion is tracked per speaker, not per name-tag — on all 8/8: relabel Human / Assistant / Person 1 / 2 and the vector barely moves (Fig 19).
  • The geometry transfers; the single-token readout does not. Reading an emotion off one word is weaker and model-dependent (scenario 5/8; user-vs-reply 0/8); averaging over the passage recovers it (Fig 2 / 10).
  • Differences track family, not size. Only Llama puts the calm↔excited axis at #2; Gemma fails single-word readouts; a 4B edge model is as clean as a 70B. None matches Claude across the board (Llama is closest on representation).

1 · What holds up across (almost) every model

  • Emotions sit on a human-like map. The biggest axis of variation always sorts emotions from unpleasant → pleasant (psychologists call this valence; terrified → hopeful). It matches human ratings on 8/8 models (correlation 0.70–0.78; Fig 7–8). Similar emotions cluster and opposites point apart (Fig 5–6), as in the paper.
  • The reply's emotion is readable at the ':' before the model answers. The activation right before the reply, at the ':' after "Assistant", already predicts the reply's emotion on all 8. Whether the ':' does so better than the user's own words (the paper's "prepared, not just echoing" gap) is clear on only 2/8 (Fig 11); on the rest the user's words predict the reply about equally well.
  • Emotion is tracked per speaker, not per name-tag. Each vector means "the current speaker's own feeling" rather than "the other person's". It barely changes if you relabel Human / Assistant / Person 1 / Person 2, yet the two speakers stay distinct (Fig 19), on all 8/8 models.
  • Each vector pushes the model toward on-theme words (afraid → "fear") across many languages (恐惧, страх), on every model (Table 1, logit lens).
  • A fixed yardstick. Claude's own 1–7 emotion ratings match published human ratings at 0.93 / 0.91 (Fig 58). The number is identical on every page; it is what justifies using Claude's ratings as a human stand-in in the geometry test.

What each column asks, in plain words:
• Scenario lights up its emotion: read at one word, does an implicit story (e.g. "my flight was cancelled") activate its emotion's vector? (count of 12 scenarios that hit their own emotion)
• Intensity moves the right way: do "more vs less intense" cues shift the emotion in the predicted direction? (count out of 12 with the correct sign; a loose bar, since ≈6/12 is chance)
• Valence is the #1 axis: correlation between the biggest axis and human pleasant↔unpleasant ratings
• Calm↔excited axis: which axis is arousal, and how strong (the paper finds it as the #2 axis)
• User ≠ reply emotion: can the user's feeling be told apart from the model's reply-feeling? (paper: yes, r ≈ 0.11; a high r means they are NOT separable)
• Reply emotion at ':': does the ':' position predict the reply's emotion better than the user's word? (green = ':' clearly wins, the paper's gap; amber = ':' predicts the reply but no better than the user's word)
• Per-speaker, not name-tag: two cosines. Own-relabel is a speaker's own-feeling vector when relabeled Human / Person 1 / Assistant-story; high means the vector ignores the name. Own-vs-other is a speaker's own feeling vs the other person's; low means the two are distinct. The paper finds both.

  • PC1 is always valence (≈26–33% of the variance, ~2× the size of PC2). On every model the single biggest axis sorts emotions pleasant↔unpleasant, a universal good-versus-bad-feeling dimension.
  • PC2 is always ≈12–14% of the variance, but its meaning differs. On the 3 Llama models PC2 is arousal (calm↔excited), the paper's textbook layout. On Gemma and Qwen PC2 is not cleanly arousal (arousal barely correlates with it); their arousal sits on a deeper axis (PC3/PC4), so PC2 there carries some other secondary structure.
  • Takeaway: the first axis (valence) is universal and strong; the second axis is where models diverge. Only Llama puts the classic arousal axis there, matching the paper.

4 · Where the models differ, from each other and the paper

  • The calm↔excited axis (arousal) isn't always the #2 axis. Only the 3 Llama models match the paper here; Gemma and Qwen put arousal on a deeper axis. See the Fig 7 close-up above for the per-model breakdown.
  • Reading the emotion off a single word is too coarse. Two tests read at one token. "Does a scenario light up its own emotion?" (Fig 2) works on 5/8 (Llama + Qwen) but fails on Gemma. "Can we tell the user's feeling from the model's reply-feeling?" (Fig 10) fails on all 8; at one word both read the same generic mood. Averaging over the whole passage recovers it, so the structure is there. The one-word snapshot just misses it.

5 · The big picture: by family, by size, vs Claude

By model family

  • Llama (3.1-8B / 3.1-70B / 3.3-70B): the most paper-faithful geometry. It is the only family where the calm↔excited (arousal) axis lands on PC2, the paper's #2 axis. The single-word scenario readout (Fig 2) is clean, and the (untied) logit lens gives the most legible English up-tokens. 3.1-70B and 3.3-70B are near-identical fine-tune twins (vectors ~0.99 cosine).
  • Qwen (3-8B / 3-32B): readouts work, with one quirk. Single-word Fig 2 is clean, unlike Gemma, and arousal sits on a deeper axis. One oddity: Qwen3-32B is the only model where the reply-emotion edge at ':' disappears (user ≈ ':').
  • Gemma (4-31B dense / E4B edge / 26B-A4B MoE): strong geometry, weak single-word readouts. Fig 2 fails (0–2/12) and arousal sits on a deep PC4. The dense, edge, and mixture-of-experts variants are indistinguishable here; architecture makes no difference, and the family signature dominates.

By size

  • The human-like representation is scale-invariant. It is just as clean in the 4B-class edge model (Gemma-E4B, width 2560) as in the 70Bs. Valence stays the #1 axis, emotions still cluster, and the match to human norms holds at every size.
  • The differences track family, not size. Arousal-on-PC2 is a Llama trait at 8B and 70B. The Fig 2 single-word failure is a Gemma trait at 31B, E4B and MoE. The small Llama-8B and Qwen-8B even read single-word scenarios better than the larger Gemma-31B; bigger is not more paper-faithful here.

vs Claude

Claude (the paper's model) shows the full set of effects: the clean representation geometry and the single-word contextual readouts. Among the 8 open models, none matches Claude across the board. Llama is closest on representation (arousal on PC2 plus the cleanest geometry), and all of them trail Claude on the single-word contextual readouts.

Same as Claude (all / most open models)

  • Emotions are clean directions on a human-like map, valence = #1 axis (all 8).
  • Similar emotions cluster; opposites point apart (all 8).
  • The reply's emotion is readable at the Assistant ':' (all 8).
  • Emotion is tracked per speaker, not per name-tag, Fig 19 (all 8/8).

Weaker than Claude

  • Arousal isn't always the #2 axis; only the 3 Llama models match (Gemma/Qwen put it deeper).
  • The ':' only clearly beats the user position (the paper's "not echoing" gap) on 2/8; elsewhere the user's words predict the reply about as well.
  • Single-word readouts are weaker (scenario 5/8; user-vs-reply 0/8); averaging recovers them.
  • Effects are generally smaller; these are smaller open models than Claude.

Why the gap (fair-comparison note). The paper studied Claude, a large frontier model with internal evals; we use 8 open-weight models and public datasets, with a simpler single-token readout. So the differences most likely reflect scale and method rather than a flaw in the paper. The pattern: the emotion representation is the easy, universal part and shows up everywhere; faithful contextual readout is where scale and family start to matter.
