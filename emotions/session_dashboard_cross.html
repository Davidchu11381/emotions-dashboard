Cross-Model Analysis

Emotion concepts across 8 open models · replication of Sofroniew et al. 2026 · updated 2026-06-03

Part A · Characterization
Part B · Behavior

In one line: we ran the identical pipeline on 8 open-weight models (Gemma-4, Qwen3, Llama-3.1/3.3) and compared the results against the paper, which studied Claude. The paper has two halves. It first characterizes the emotion representations (what they are, how they form, how they are read off), then shows how they drive behavior (preferences, and misaligned actions like reward hacking). This page mirrors that split: Part A · Characterization (here) and Part B · Behavior (next). Part A in short: the way models represent emotions carries over well; reading an emotion off a single word is weaker and depends on the model.

The setup is identical for every model. For each one we extract one "emotion vector" per emotion (its average middle-layer activation over short emotion-evoking stories, denoised against neutral text), then run the paper's tests, so this page compares results only. Verdicts: green = matches the paper · amber = partly · red = no. Every number is the model's own; per-model method details live on each model's page.

★ Key findings — across all 8 models

Bottom line: the way these models represent emotions replicates broadly; what's weaker is the paper's single-word read of the emotion — on these smaller models that one word is too noisy, and averaging over the whole passage recovers what it misses.

vs Claude — likely why: the representation geometry is about as clean as the paper's Claude; the gap is the single-word read. A frontier model like Claude concentrates the emotion at that one word; these smaller, lower-capacity models leave it spread across the passage, so reading one word is noisy — user-vs-reply emotion fails on all 8, and the ':' beats the user's word on only 2/8.

  • Emotions form a human-like map on every model. The top axis is always valence (unpleasant↔pleasant), matching human ratings on 8/8 (r 0.70–0.78); similar emotions cluster, opposites point apart (Fig 5–8).
  • The reply's emotion is set before the model speaks. Readable at the Assistant ':' on all 8; it clearly beats the user's own words on only 2/8 (Fig 11).
  • Emotion is tracked per speaker, not per name-tag — on all 8/8: relabel Human / Assistant / Person 1 / 2 and the vector barely moves (Fig 19).
  • Each vector points at on-theme words, in many languages — afraid → fear, 恐惧, страх — on all 8 (Table 1, logit lens).
  • The geometry transfers; the single-word read does not. At one word the emotion is weak and model-dependent (scenario 5/8; user-vs-reply 0/8) — it is there in the passage, just not readable from any single word (Fig 2 / 10).
  • Differences track family, not size. Only Llama puts the calm↔excited axis at #2; Gemma can't read it from a single word; a 4B edge model is as clean as a 70B. None matches Claude across the board (Llama is closest on representation).

What each column asks, in plain words:
• Scenario lights up its emotion: read at one word, does an implicit story (e.g. "my flight was cancelled") activate its emotion's vector? (count of 12 scenarios that hit their own emotion)
• Intensity moves the right way: do "more vs less intense" cues shift the emotion in the predicted direction? (count out of 12 with the correct sign; a loose bar, since ≈6/12 is chance)
• Valence is the #1 axis: correlation between the biggest axis and human pleasant↔unpleasant ratings
• Calm↔excited axis: which axis is arousal, and how strong (the paper finds it as the #2 axis)
• User ≠ reply emotion: can the user's feeling be told apart from the model's reply-feeling? (paper: yes, r ≈ 0.11; a high r means they are NOT separable)
• Reply emotion at ':': does the ':' position predict the reply's emotion better than the user's word? (green = ':' clearly wins, the paper's gap; amber = ':' predicts the reply but no better than the user's word)
• Per-speaker, not name-tag: two cosines. Own-relabel is a speaker's own-feeling vector when relabeled Human / Person 1 / Assistant-story; high means the vector ignores the name. Own-vs-other is a speaker's own feeling vs the other person's; low means the two are distinct. The paper finds both.
The cell color is the verdict: green = matches the paper  ·  amber = present but weaker, or on a deeper axis  ·  red = does not match.

3 · By family, and vs Claude

  • Llama (3.1-8B / 3.1-70B / 3.3-70B) — most paper-faithful. The only family with arousal on PC2, the cleanest single-word read, and the most legible (untied) logit lens. 3.1-70B and 3.3-70B are near-identical fine-tune twins (vectors ~0.99 cosine).
  • Qwen (3-8B / 3-32B) — single-word reads work, one quirk. Single-word Fig 2 is clean and arousal sits on a deeper axis. Qwen3-32B is the only model where the ':' edge over the user disappears.
  • Gemma (4-31B / E4B / 26B-A4B) — strong geometry, weak single-word reads. Fig 2 fails (0–2/12), arousal on a deep PC4; dense, edge, and MoE are indistinguishable, so family beats both architecture and size.
  • vs Claude: Claude shows the full set — clean geometry and the single-word reads. None of the 8 open models matches it across the board; Llama is closest (geometry), and all trail on reading the emotion from one word.

Fair-comparison note. The paper studied Claude (a large frontier model, internal evals); we use 8 open-weight models, public data, and we read the emotion at a single word rather than averaging over the passage — so the gaps most likely reflect scale and method, not a flaw in the paper. (Human-norm benchmark: Claude's own 1–7 ratings match published human valence/arousal norms at 0.93 / 0.91, Fig 58 — which is why they serve as the human stand-in.)
